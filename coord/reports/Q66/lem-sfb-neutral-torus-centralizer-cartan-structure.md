## Review by Q66

I checked the item against the Milne passages listed above, including rank zero, positive characteristic, and scheme rather than rational-point centralizers. Its hypotheses are sufficient and its clauses are unchanged. There is no statement dispute. No SGA source is used. No new node requires an additional source or edge-case audit.

For step 1 I opened the closed-factorization-locus, affine-source-separatedness, and torus-fixed-scheme-smooth statements. The first quantifies over every base scheme, so its application with Y=T, S=G and the diagonal really represents the universal centralizer condition, not just commutation on k-points. All schemes here are separated finite type: they are affine, and the affine-source separatedness lemma supplies the closed diagonal. The fixed-scheme theorem assumes a representing closed subscheme; the preceding construction supplies exactly that hypothesis.

For step 2 I checked the component-translates statement gives an open-and-closed subgroup with the open scheme structure, not merely a connected set. The smooth-restriction statement applies to the structural map of the smooth centralizer. A split torus has domain Laurent coordinate ring (k itself in rank zero), so it is connected and its map lands in the identity component; the open-factorization lemma gives the factorization as a scheme map. Centrality holds on all algebras from the centralizer functor, and maximality in C follows from maximality in G without another theorem.

For step 3 I checked the central-maximal-torus-nilpotent statement explicitly includes both the central-series conclusion and the terminating scheme-theoretic commutator conclusion. C satisfies its smoothness, connectedness and central maximal-torus hypotheses. Its central series also supplies the commutative-quotient formulation of solvability used by perfect-solvable-unipotent-torus. The field is perfect because it is algebraically closed. That existing statement really supplies U smooth, connected, normal, unipotent and a torus fppf quotient; a definition of unipotence alone would not suffice.

For step 4 I read affine-normal-quotient-geometry's scheme-theoretic-kernel clause and solvable-torus-complements' assertion that q restricted to every maximal torus is an isomorphism. Both apply to the same C,U,T and quotient, with the same fppf meaning. I wrote both the multiplication map and its inverse using tau, checked q kills the first inverse coordinate, and checked the products after arbitrary algebra extensions. Centrality is exactly what turns the semidirect product law into the direct product law. This step does not invoke a product theorem as an unexplained identification.

For step 5 I read geometric-unipotence-unitriangular-embedding and diagonalizable-to-unipotent-trivial. The second theorem assumes an actual subgroup of the unitriangular group; the first supplies it for smooth U. Its source D_k(M) includes M=Z^a, which is the Laurent-polynomial presentation of any split torus S. Thus the projection of S to U is zero as a scheme homomorphism, not merely at rational points. The inverse product formula then factors S through T. Applying this to an automorphism and its inverse gives equality of the torus images. No representability of an automorphism group is assumed.

For step 6 I checked central-torus-faithful-weight-blocks permits H=G with the rank-zero central torus, so it supplies a faithful representation of the AMBIENT group G. This matters: semisimplicity in the statement is for representations of G, not arbitrary representations of C. The G_m weight-basis theorem applies successively to the commuting factors of the split T; preservation of earlier eigenspaces is an immediate commutation calculation. Rational-representation-unipotent-point really transfers unipotence from U's faithful realization to the restriction of this ambient representation, with no connectedness hypothesis needed there. Multiplicative-jordan-linear-compatibility explicitly states uniqueness of the commuting diagonalizable/unipotent factorization. Those are exactly rho(t) and rho(u). A diagonalizable rho(s) has unipotent part 1, and faithfulness gives u=1. I corrected the tempting but invalid shortcut of using a faithful representation of C without checking ambient semisimplicity.

For step 7 I checked the closed-factorization locus again, now for C conjugated by a parameter in G. Intersecting the forward and inverse loci imposes equality of subgroup functors after every extension, so P(k) is exactly N in the item. The locally-algebraic-group-identity-component statement supplies a genuine smooth connected reduced subgroup M with the same underlying space as P^circ. The open-factorization lemma handles C into P^circ; smooth-geometrically-reduced makes C reduced, so nilpotents pull back to zero and the map factors through M. This avoids assuming the whole normalizer is smooth or using a result whose proof depends on the item.

For step 8 I checked the scheme-versus-point distinction explicitly. Uniqueness of T initially gives its invariance only for each point m in M(k). I used the closed-factorization locus with parameter M to encode invariance as a closed subscheme Z of M, and the vanishing part (2) of affine-nullstellensatz-vanishing, not a topological assertion, to make its ideal zero. Its hypotheses hold: M is reduced, affine, finite type over algebraically closed k. Thus there is a scheme action M on T; the connected-action-torus-rigidity theorem applies because M is smooth and geometrically connected (over this algebraically closed field). Its conclusion says the action is trivial over every algebra, which supplies the full centralizer factorization. Connectedness then factors M through C. Together with step 7's inclusion this proves M=C scheme-theoretically.

For step 9 I reread component-translates' finiteness and translation clauses, which apply even when P is nonreduced. Every field-valued point factors through reduction, so P^circ(k)=M(k)=C(k), and each connected component's k-points form exactly one coset. There is no inference from topological equality to equality of nonreduced subgroup schemes. Inversion also reconciles either convention for the words right coset.
