## Review by Q01

I checked the unchanged hypothesis and conclusion against Conrad, Proposition 3.1.9 proof (1), printed p.88, specifically the paragraph beginning with the central torus. It forms a nonsolvable smooth connected affine quotient of dimension three, and then excludes a nontrivial unipotent radical because the remaining quotient has dimension at most two. My proof follows that route but tests an arbitrary nontrivial smooth connected normal unipotent subgroup directly, so it does not need a new existence theorem for a largest radical. The local Milne sources I read were printed pp.104–108 (5.23–5.29 and 5.39), pp.131–132 (6.27 and 6.30), pp.282–285 (14.7, 14.12, 14.18 and 14.21), and p.360 (17.27). Remark 5.42, printed pp.108–109, was also read online. These support the existing nodes used; I did not cite SGA as a source.

Step 1 uses the statement of `lem-sfb-affine-normal-quotient-geometry` for representability, faithful flatness, kernel, smoothness and connectedness of H/Z. Centrality gives normality directly on every algebra of sections. The field is algebraically closed, so the geometric-connectedness clause in `lem-sfb-solvable-normal-subgroup-products` applies. `lem-sfb-group-hom-dimension-kernel-image` supplies exactly the equality dim H = dim Q + dim Z, with finite integer dimensions; subtraction gives three. Neither quotient smoothness nor the dimension equality was taken merely from a definition.

Step 2 uses `lem-sfb-smooth-connected-derived-subgroup` for existence and smooth connectedness of successive derived subgroups, and `lem-sfb-affine-normal-quotient-geometry` for the quotients used to test the commutator factorizations. The induction that homomorphisms carry derived terms into derived terms is written from the commutator formula and the minimality in the definition. A derived term of H then lies in the commutative kernel Z; the next term is trivial. This is the elementary two-line central-extension argument, not an uncited theorem asserting extension-closure of solvability.

Step 3 uses `lem-sc-group-scheme-separated-field` only as a generic tool to check separatedness for the factorization theorem; the requested off-unit tool use is declared in split_report. The statement of `lem-sfb-algebraic-group-homomorphism-factorization` supplies the faithfully flat map N to I and closed image I in Q. The statement of `lem-sfb-unipotent-normal-quotient-image` applies because q is faithfully flat and N is smooth connected normal unipotent: it explicitly gives smoothness, connectedness, normality and unipotence of I with no separability assumption. If I is trivial, the kernel property factors N into Z, and `lem-sfb-unipotent-to-multiplicative-trivial` states exactly that this homomorphism must vanish. Its smooth geometric-point convention agrees with the item's unipotence convention. The subgroup inclusion can be trivial only for N=1, on all algebras. I therefore need no assertion that the scheme-theoretic intersection N with Z is smooth. The strict dimension inequality from `lem-sfb-solvable-normal-subgroup-products` applies to the proper smooth connected inclusion 1 into I; it supplies positive dimension without a hidden assertion about infinitesimal groups.

Step 4 applies the same quotient-geometry and dimension statements to Q/I. I is a closed normal subgroup by step 3. Q is smooth connected affine of finite type, so the quotient R has all hypotheses of `lem-sfb-connected-group-dimension-two-solvable`. That node explicitly states solvability in the derived-series sense in every characteristic when dim R is at most two. The equality 3 = dim I + dim R and dim I at least one give that bound, including the possible zero-dimensional quotient.

Step 5 uses `lem-sfb-smooth-unipotent-solvable` for a finite commutative-quotient series of I, and uses the correspondence clause of `lem-sfb-affine-normal-quotient-geometry` for the exact quotients in the pulled-back series from R. `lem-sfb-smooth-connected-derived-subgroup` supplies the derived terms. Concatenating the two finite series and inducting D^jQ into their terms proves solvability in precisely the item's derived-series sense; this also checks the apparent difference between the normal-series convention in the unipotent lemma and the derived-series convention here. I wrote this argument rather than treating a definition node as a theorem about solvability.
