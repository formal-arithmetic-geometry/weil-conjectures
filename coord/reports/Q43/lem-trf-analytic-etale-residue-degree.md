Step 1 uses the completion lemma's actual henselianity sentence, not an argument hidden in its proof. Its auxiliary field extension may be F/F, which is finite separable even when H/F has not been shown separable. Completeness identifies F with its completion by taking limits, preserving the given norm. The elementary ring, unit and ideal checks show A and B are valuation domains, identify their fraction fields and residue fields, and check that the map is local. Thus the hypothesis needed in step 2 is present.

Step 2 uses the actual local-factor statement with R=A, D=B and the maximal ideal n. Since B is already local, B_n=B. The cited statement gives finite separable residue extension and equality between fraction-field and residue-field degrees. Its hypotheses include henselianity and etaleness and allow a nondiscrete valuation domain; all are in force here. No assertion that arbitrary B is finite over A has been smuggled into the forward proof.

Step 3 uses the finite-separable primitive-element statement for l/k and the simple-extension power-basis statement for k(alpha). Each cited node was opened and does say that. A representative u in B exists by the definition of the residue quotient; u is not presumed to satisfy a lifted polynomial. Degree d is positive because these are fields, including the identity extension.

Step 4 proves, rather than cites, the norm identity: normalize any nonzero coefficient tuple by a coefficient attaining its finite maximum. The normalized residues are in k and one is 1. Their combination cannot vanish against the residue power basis. A nonzero residue in B means norm exactly 1, so multiplication by the normalizing coefficient gives the identity. This is an elementary finite-sum calculation; there is no missing density argument and no hidden approximation lemma.

Step 5 applies finite-dimensional linear algebra to d independent vectors in a d-dimensional space. The norm identity then proves that b lies in B exactly when all its coordinates lie in A. Applying this to u^d constructs a monic g with coefficients in A; reduction and independence identify its reduction with the minimal polynomial of alpha. The separability definition is cited because it explicitly defines separability by coprimality of that minimal polynomial and its derivative. Evaluating the Bezout identity gives nonzero derivative residue, hence a unit. All of this follows in the written calculation from the preceding basis and the cited definition, so this step is elementary. It is not a new theorem restating one clause of the item.

Step 6 uses exactly the monic-quotient power basis supplied by the cited node and the etaleness assertion of the cited standard-derivative node. Evaluation identifies A[T]/(g) with B by its explicit basis; the derivative is a unit by step 5, so adjoining its inverse changes nothing. The displayed mutually inverse maps identify B with the standard etale algebra, which proves the reverse implication. No unproved preservation or comparison assertion is hidden in the word identification.

I tested the identity extension (d=1, even alpha=0 is permitted), an unramified extension of a p-adic field, a nontrivial totally ramified extension with unchanged residue field, and a purely inseparable residue extension. Ramified examples fail degree equality, and inseparable examples fail the separability/derivative hypothesis; neither is accidentally proved etale. The finite-maximum argument works in dense as well as discrete value groups. Every non-elementary use has a verbatim quotation in citations.json. No step exceeds 2000 characters.
