Original step 1 — Helper A:

Put $P=\pi_0(T)$ and $Q=\pi_0(S)$. If $C$ is a connected component of $T$, then $f(C)$ is connected: a separation of its image by relatively open sets would pull back to a separation of $C$. Thus $f(C)$ lies in a unique connected component of $S$. Define $h:P\to Q$ by that component. This is independent of the representative, and $h q_T=q_S f$ holds pointwise. For an open $W\subseteq Q$, the set $q_T^{-1}(h^{-1}(W))=f^{-1}(q_S^{-1}(W))$ is open. The quotient topology on $P$ therefore makes $h$ continuous. Surjectivity of $q_T$ also proves uniqueness of a map satisfying the normalization.

Original step 2 — Existing spectral-component and clopen-basis nodes:

Both $T$ and $S$ are spectral by hypothesis. Apply [lem-pe-spectral-components-profinite] to each: $P$ and $Q$ are compact Hausdorff totally disconnected spaces. In particular the clopen-basis theorem [lem-weil-pbc-profinite-clopen-basis] applies to both $P$ and $Q$. No assertion that individual components of $T$ or $S$ are open is needed.

Original step 3 — Helper A:

For any space $Z$, every clopen subset $U$ is a union of connected components: a component meeting both $U$ and its complement would be separated by their intersections with it. Consequently $q_Z^{-1}(q_Z(U))=U$. The quotient topology shows that $q_Z(U)$ is clopen, since its preimage and the preimage of its complement are open. Conversely the preimage of a clopen subset under $q_Z$ is clopen, by the same quotient-topology criterion. As $q_Z$ is surjective, these operations are inverse bijections between $\operatorname{Clop}(Z)$ and $\operatorname{Clop}(\pi_0(Z))$. The normalization in step 1 gives $q_T^{-1}(h^{-1}(V))=f^{-1}(q_S^{-1}(V))$ for every clopen $V\subseteq Q$. Conjugating the assumed bijection $f^{-1}$ by these two quotient-preimage bijections proves that $h^{-1}:\operatorname{Clop}(Q)\to\operatorname{Clop}(P)$ is bijective.

Original step 4 — Helper B:

To prove injectivity, suppose $a,b\in P$ are distinct. As $P$ is Hausdorff, $P\setminus\{b\}$ is an open neighbourhood of $a$. By [lem-weil-pbc-profinite-clopen-basis], choose a clopen $U$ with $a\in U\subseteq P\setminus\{b\}$. Surjectivity of the clopen inverse-image map in step 3 gives a clopen $V\subseteq Q$ with $U=h^{-1}(V)$. Then $h(a)\in V$ and $h(b)\notin V$, so $h(a)\ne h(b)$. Thus $h$ is injective.

Original step 5 — Helper B:

To prove surjectivity, suppose $y\in Q$ is outside $h(P)$. For every $x\in P$, the open set $Q\setminus\{y\}$ contains $h(x)$, since $Q$ is Hausdorff and $h(x)\ne y$. By [lem-weil-pbc-profinite-clopen-basis], choose a clopen $V_x$ with $h(x)\in V_x$ and $y\notin V_x$. The open sets $h^{-1}(V_x)$ cover compact $P$, so finitely many suffice. Their corresponding finite union $V\subseteq Q$ is clopen, contains $h(P)$, and excludes $y$. Hence $h^{-1}(V)=P=h^{-1}(Q)$. Injectivity of the clopen inverse-image map from step 3 gives $V=Q$, contrary to $y\notin V$. Therefore $h$ is surjective. This includes $P=\varnothing$: the finite subcover can be empty, so $V=\varnothing$ and the same contradiction applies to any proposed $y\in Q$.

Original step 6 — Helper B:

For a clopen $U\subseteq P$, step 3 gives a clopen $V\subseteq Q$ with $U=h^{-1}(V)$. Bijectivity of $h$ implies $h(U)=V$: one inclusion is immediate, and for $y\in V$ choose $x$ with $h(x)=y$, obtaining $x\in U$. Every open subset $O\subseteq P$ is a union of clopen subsets $U\subseteq O$ by [lem-weil-pbc-profinite-clopen-basis]. Its image is the union of the open sets $h(U)$, so $h$ is open. The inverse of the continuous bijection $h$ is therefore continuous, since its inverse image of any open $O\subseteq P$ is $h(O)$. Thus $h=\pi_0(f)$ is a homeomorphism with precisely the normalization in step 1. If $Q$ is empty then the map $h:P\to Q$ forces $P$ empty; together with step 5 this covers all empty-space cases.
