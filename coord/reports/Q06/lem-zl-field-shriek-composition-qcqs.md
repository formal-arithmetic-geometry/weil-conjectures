Step 2 names the actual strict semilinear reduction/limit functors from `lem-coef-derived-system-evaluation-calculus`, not just a collection of finite objects. The integral definition imposes one cohomology interval for all reductions but does not itself impose Tor amplitude. I therefore read and cited `lem-coef-integral-residue-tor`: bounded residue reduction gives a common Tor interval for every quotient. That is precisely what the realization theorem requires. `lem-sf-proetale-constructible-origin` clause (ii) gives the bounded étale-origin witnesses, not merely an equivalence for sheaves. Integral compact-support constructibility applies to arbitrary finite-type schemes over a field and separated finite-type maps. Applying it to compactifications `(j,1)` and `(1,p)` verifies preservation by the open and proper generators. The operation-reduction statement covers their actual finite reductions and finite-Tor transition comparisons. Induction on the finite word, with the original integral input as base and these open/proper cases as step, verifies the intermediate bounds and normalized systems. There is no assertion that arbitrary bounded constructible complexes over the nonregular quotient ring are perfect; the Tor conclusion is specific to reductions of the integral input/output.

Step 3 cites `lem-sf-integral-compactification-system-realization` for an actual derived semilinear proper/open map, its open-adjunction normalization, finite evaluations and pasting identities. I checked those clauses in its statement, including its explicit warning against arbitrary lifts of finite arrows. The strict-tower proper/open statement gives invertibility of the normalized finite comparisons for bounded-below étale-origin coefficients. Each $A_n$ is finite, and step 2 gives precisely those origin witnesses. The system-evaluation calculus says a system map is an isomorphism if all its evaluations are isomorphisms. That is sufficient here: evaluation is used to prove invertibility of the given map, never faithfulness or equality of derived maps. This also applies to the noncartesian refinement squares used later, since the strict-tower statement explicitly covers them.

Step 4 writes the comparison as the particular word $S_qJ_iS_{p_Y}J_c\to S_{qp}J_a$. The realization theorem's statement explicitly covers finite words, nested compact-support functors, quotient transitions, proper/open maps and whiskering. Thus it genuinely identifies the two functors of this item and the requested map; this is not an appeal to something contained only in that node's proof. The definition of integral/rational/common-stage compact support fixes the same quotient-induced transitions. The expression $c_{S_{qp}J_a}\mathcal L(A)c^{-1}_{S_qJ_iS_{p_Y}J_c}$ and its inverse spell out what “pass to the integral limit” means.

Step 5 uses the cofiltered-compactifications statement only after existence, and the compatible-tower statement for common refining towers. The formal refinement-coherence theorem assumes the proper/open data; it does not prove their existence or invertibility. Those are supplied by steps 1–3 and the realization theorem. Its actual conclusion is the common-refinement compatibility for exactly the word used in step 4. Its hypotheses on pasting are identities of derived systems from the realization statement, so applying the limit functor preserves them without assuming finite evaluations faithful. Repeated compatible towers provide the stated staircase hypothesis. This is why canonicity is established before rationalizing.
