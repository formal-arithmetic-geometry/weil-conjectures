For the division input the coefficient computation is as follows (recorded here, not as a proof of the new unsplit node). For f of leading term a F^s and g of leading term b F^t, s>=t, subtract c F^(s-t) g with c=a/sigma^(s-t)(b) to lower degree. To divide on the other side subtract g (c F^(s-t)) with c=sigma^(-t)(b^(-1)a). This uses surjectivity of sigma precisely on that side. Each nonzero remainder has strictly smaller degree. Induction on degree proves the two algorithms; constant g leaves zero remainder. This is the actual calculation behind Milne p.296, stated for a general automorphism.

For the matrix input the proof left for its future worker is a genuine matrix lemma, not the item's cyclic conclusion. Among all nonzero entries of all matrices reachable from C by finitely many invertible row and column operations, choose one of least delta, and move it to (1,1). The set of possible deltas is a nonempty subset of N. If an entry a in column 1 is qb+r, subtract q times the pivot row from its row. A nonzero remainder would be an entry of a reachable matrix with smaller delta, a contradiction. Thus the column clears. For an entry a in row 1 write a=bq'+r' and subtract column 1 times q' from its column. Again r'=0 by minimality, and the already zero entries below the pivot mean these operations keep column 1 cleared. The result is block diagonal with b and a smaller rectangular block. Induct on min(m,n); the empty and zero-matrix cases are immediate. Extend the smaller block's invertible operations by identity on the pivot coordinate. Products of these invertible matrices give U and V. No claim about divisibility among diagonal entries is used, and no determinant, domain, commutativity, or delta(ab) formula is needed. This is an independent finite-matrix input, strictly smaller than the module theorem: it says nothing about modules, their finite presentations, or quotients.

## Source error

The item already omits Milne 14.51's words "or irreducible"; I retain that correction and do not restore the false assertion. For k=F_p, Frobenius is identity, so A=F_p[F] and M=A/(F^2) is a counterexample: F acts nontrivially but F^2 acts by zero. A direct sum of free modules or quotients by irreducible polynomials cannot have this action. There are no free factors because F^2 annihilates the whole module; the irreducibles on all remaining nonzero factors would have to divide F^2 and hence be F, making F act by zero, a contradiction. Proposition 14.52 uses arbitrary g, exactly the corrected form in this item. No target statement or source route has been weakened by this delivery.
