## Argument behind the repaired statement-only helpers

This records why I believe the new statements, without splitting them a second level or placing proofs in their statements. For the valuation helper, if k=v_l(n), then n>=l^k>=1+k(l-1), giving k<=(n-1)/(l-1). Legendre's formula gives `(l-1) v_l(n!)=n-s_l(n)<=n-1` for n>=1. Substituting these two bounds gives `nr-e v >= r+(n-1)delta` for both denominators. Complete nonarchimedean fields sum any sequence tending to zero; the same tail bound is uniform on the closed disc. Finite polynomial sums are continuous and preserve the closed ideal, yielding continuity and the asserted domains.

For the substitution helper, a restricted coefficient sequence is bounded. For fixed n the product family in n indices has only finitely many terms of norm above any positive epsilon, since each weighted inner sequence tends to zero. The bound `|a_n|s^n ->0` makes all sufficiently large n contribute no such terms, uniformly in the inner indices. The union over the finitely many remaining n is finite. Completeness and the ultrametric inequality make this countable expanded family summable. Grouping by n gives F(G(x)); grouping by total degree gives the formal composition's evaluated series. The same finite-above-epsilon argument applied to weighted coefficients gives restrictedness of the composed coefficients: degrees exceeding a bound include no large expanded term, and finite coefficient sums cannot increase the maximum norm. For products use two restricted bounded coefficient sequences and the same argument on pairs of indices. This proves both clauses with the explicit norm bound, including n=0. It is the additional supremum/radius condition, not just convergence of the values, that makes the proof work.

For the purely formal helper the familiar derivative argument is coefficientwise: L'(T)=1/(1+T) and (1+A)'=1+A. Hence (L(A(T)))'=1 and its constant term is 0, so L(A(T))=T in a rational algebra. L has linear coefficient 1, so substitution by L is injective by the first differing coefficient; composing L(A)=T with L and cancelling this substitution gives A(L)=T. This is algebra in formal series and requires no analytic rearrangement assumption.
