I read the item, its statement dependency def-sfb-finite-infinite-set, its parent, the latest blueprint places above, and Cashen's pp.27–29. The finite-set dependency defines exactly the finite predicate in the statement; group, subgroup, order, binary words and the length function are defined inline. All hypotheses used are the original ones: s,t distinct, both nonidentity, both square to 1, and m the least positive exponent killing st. No deletion condition, reflection action, finiteness of G, presentation, or Coxeter structure is assumed.

Step 1 uses the definition of least positive order. The Euclidean division n=qm+j works for negative integers too, so exponent equality modulo m is actually proved rather than quoted as an uncited group lemma. Step 2 uses srs=r^{-1} and cancellation. If s were a power of r it would commute with r, forcing r^2=1; m=2 follows, and its two possible powers make either s or t trivial. Thus parity separation is established even when m=2; no determinant or parity character is smuggled in.

Step 3 just pairs adjacent letters and uses t=r^{-1}s. I checked all four formulas with the beginning-letter convention; Mathlib alternatingWord instead uses an ending-letter convention. For m even the exponent difference is 2a=m, and for m odd it is a-(-a-1)=m, giving the same length-m product without assuming a braid relation.

Step 4 uses only steps 1–3, not an external node. Same-parity same-start collisions have exponent difference |k-l|/2<m. Opposite-start collisions have difference (k+l)/2, positive unless both are the empty word, and at most m. Its only possible nonzero multiple of m is m, forcing k=l=m. Opposite parities cannot collide by step 2. This covers every collision, the identity, and both parities of m.

Step 5 gives the actual shortening move, including the boundary letter after the replaced block. Swapping a length-m alternating block flips its final letter for either parity; the next letter of the old alternating word was precisely the other letter, so a new equal pair can be deleted. Adjacent cancellations and this replacement each shorten by exactly two. Step 6 applies these explicit moves until length cannot decrease; the terminal word has length at most m and alternates. No separate graph, normal-form, confluence, cardinality or exchange theorem is being invoked.

Step 7 uses the well ordering of natural lengths and the already proved collision and shortening calculations. A shortest word cannot admit either shortening move; hence it is among the words classified in step 4. This proves minimality and uniqueness for every length below m, exactly two shortest words at m, and the unique empty representative of length zero. The two length-m words are distinct because s differs from t.
