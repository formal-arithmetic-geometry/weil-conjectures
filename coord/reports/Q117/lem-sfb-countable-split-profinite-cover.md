### Old Step 1

Let $V=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}$ with the product topology, and embed it order-preservingly in the usual Cantor subset of $[0,1]$ by $c(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}2x_n3^{-n-1}$; $c$ is continuous and injective, so the lexicographic order on $V$ is the order induced by $c$. Let $D\subset V$ be the set of sequences that are eventually equal to the periodic sequence $0101\ldots$. It is countably infinite, every nonempty basic clopen cylinder $\{x:x_0\ldots x_{m-1}=w\}$ contains the infinitely many points $w0^r(01)^\infty$, $r\ge0$, of $D$, and no point of $D$ is eventually constant. Enumerate $D=\{v_k:k\ge0\}$ without repetition. Put $a_k=2^{-k-3}$, so $\sum_k a_k=1/4$ and the $a_k$ are strictly decreasing. The Cantor space is nonempty, compact Hausdorff, metrizable, and totally disconnected. Since $D$ is countable and $V$ has no isolated points, $V\setminus D$ is dense. Each $v_k$ has points of $V\setminus D$ approaching it from both lexicographic sides: $v_k$ has infinitely many coordinates equal to $1$ and infinitely many equal to $0$, so changing a late coordinate $1$ to $0$ (resp. $0$ to $1$) and then choosing the remaining tail outside the countable set $D$ gives points of $V\setminus D$ arbitrarily close to $v_k$ that are lexicographically smaller (resp. larger). (Eventually constant points such as $w10^\infty$ would not do: it has the immediate predecessor $w01^\infty$ and is approached from the right only.)

### Old Step 2

For $x\in V$ with $x\neq v_k$ define $h_k(x)=a_k$ when $c(x)>c(v_k)$ and $h_k(x)=-a_k$ when $c(x)<c(v_k)$; $h_k$ is locally constant on $V\setminus\{v_k\}$ because $c$ is continuous. Define $f(x)=\sum_{k\ge0}h_k(x)$ for $x\in V\setminus D$. The series converges uniformly because $|h_k|\le a_k$. Hence $f$ is continuous at every $x\notin D$: each summand is continuous at $x$, and a finite initial segment is continuous there while the uniformly small tail is bounded by $\sum_{k>M}a_k$. At $v_k$, the sum $g_k=\sum_{j\ne k}h_j(v_k)$ is well-defined; the two one-sided limits of $f$ along points of $V\setminus D$ (which exist on both sides by Step 1) are $g_k-a_k$ and $g_k+a_k$. The first assertion follows by the same finite-head/uniform-tail argument, and the two limits are distinct by $a_k>0$.

### Old Step 3

Let $T$ be the closure in $V\times[-1/4,1/4]$ of the graph $\Gamma_f=\{(x,f(x)):x\in V\setminus D\}$, and let $q:T\to V$ be the first projection. The closure is compact Hausdorff and metrizable. Since $V\setminus D$ is dense in $V$, every $v\in V$ is the limit of base points of graph points, so $q$ is surjective. For $v\notin D$, continuity of $f$ at $v$ makes the fibre exactly $\{(v,f(v))\}$. For $v=v_k$, the two one-sided sequences from Step 2 give the points $t_k^-=(v_k,g_k-a_k)$ and $t_k^+=(v_k,g_k+a_k)$ in the fibre. Conversely, any convergent sequence of graph points over bases tending to $v_k$ has, after taking a side-subsequence, one of those two limits; thus the fibre is exactly these two points.

### Old Step 4

The space $T$ is totally disconnected. Indeed, if $C\subseteq T$ is connected, then $q(C)$ is connected in the totally disconnected space $V$, hence is a singleton; $C$ is therefore contained in one fibre of $q$, which has one or two points, and a finite subspace of the Hausdorff space $T$ is discrete. Thus every connected subset is a singleton. Consequently $T$ is a nonempty metrizable profinite space, and $V$ is one as well.

### Old Step 5

Put $R=T\times_VT$. The only off-diagonal points of $R$ are the ordered pairs $(t_k^-,t_k^+)$ and $(t_k^+,t_k^-)$, by Step 3. Fix $k$ and let $I^-=(g_k-\tfrac32a_k,\,g_k-\tfrac12a_k)$ and $I^+=(g_k+\tfrac12a_k,\,g_k+\tfrac32a_k)$; they are disjoint open intervals containing the heights $g_k-a_k$ and $g_k+a_k$ of $t_k^-$ and $t_k^+$, and every height in $I^+$ exceeds every height in $I^-$ by more than $a_k$ and less than $3a_k$. Let $W=\big((V\times I^-)\cap T\big)\times\big((V\times I^+)\cap T\big)$, an open neighbourhood of $(t_k^-,t_k^+)$ in $T\times T$. If $(x,y)\in W\cap R$, then $x\ne y$ because $I^-\cap I^+=\emptyset$, so $(x,y)$ lies over some $v_j$ and $\{x,y\}=\{t_j^-,t_j^+\}$, whose heights differ by exactly $2a_j$. Hence $a_k<2a_j<3a_k$, so $a_k/2<a_j<3a_k/2$; since $a_j=2^{k-j}a_k$, this forces $j=k$, and then the height condition forces $(x,y)=(t_k^-,t_k^+)$. Thus $W\cap R=\{(t_k^-,t_k^+)\}$ is open in $R$; it is closed because $R$ is Hausdorff. The same argument with the two factors exchanged applies to $(t_k^+,t_k^-)$.

### Old Step 6

Finally let $U\subseteq T$ be nonempty open. The graph is dense in $T$, so $U$ contains a graph point $(x,f(x))$ with $x\notin D$. The fibre over $x$ is the singleton just found, hence $x\notin q(T\setminus U)$. Since $T\setminus U$ is compact and $V$ is Hausdorff, $q(T\setminus U)$ is closed; therefore $B=V\setminus q(T\setminus U)$ is a nonempty open neighbourhood of $x$ and every fibre over $B$ is contained in $U$. By the choice of the sequence $(v_k)$, for every $N$ some $k\ge N$ has $v_k\in B$. Its whole fibre, namely $\{t_k^-,t_k^+\}$, lies in $U$. This proves the required tail-density assertion.
