## Source error

The item statement is true with its existing n invertible in k hypothesis. No STATEMENT-DISPUTED correction is needed.

Milne I 14.4(a)'s proof says End(A) tensor R is compact. That whole vector space is generally noncompact (already End(E) tensor R = R for an elliptic curve without CM). The intended compact set is the sphere Tr(x-dagger x) = 2g for the positive definite Rosati form, intersected with the discrete endomorphism lattice. The theorem quoted by the new finiteness node is correct.

The end of the printed proof of I 14.4(b) jumps from nilpotence of beta to nilpotence of beta-dagger beta without stating why. That implication is false for an arbitrary nilpotent: in M_2(R) with transpose involution, beta = [[0,1],[0,0]] is nilpotent but beta-transpose beta = diag(0,1) is not. In the source's actual situation beta is normal, since a-dagger = a inverse and beta=(a-1)/n; this would repair that argument. Our general integral-ring lemma instead finishes using that a finite-order operator in characteristic zero is semisimple. Thus the proof follows the source's finite-order/divisibility/eigenvalue route while making its final inference rigorous. The item's proof_origin is marked agent because of this reformulation.

## Proof intended for the new general algebra input

This is explanatory material for the next worker; the node itself contains a statement only. If R has rank zero, it is the zero ring and the assertion is immediate. Otherwise take the left regular representation on its finite free additive group. Left multiplication by b is an integer matrix B, so every complex eigenvalue eta of B is an algebraic integer. Left multiplication by u is U=I+nB; U^r=I. Therefore zeta=1+n eta is a root of unity. Milne I 14.5 gives zeta=1. To spell out that arithmetic lemma, if zeta is nontrivial, take a power zeta' of prime order p. Then (zeta'-1)/n is still integral, since it is eta times a geometric sum. It lies in Q(zeta'), and taking field norms gives n^(p-1) dividing p, impossible for n at least three. Consequently all eigenvalues of U are one. Its minimal polynomial divides both (X-1)^d (by Cayley-Hamilton, where d is the rank) and X^r-1. In characteristic zero the latter has a simple root at one, so their gcd is X-1, and U=I. Evaluate U at 1_R to obtain u=1_R. This uses no commutativity of R. In particular it applies to matrix rings and endomorphism rings, including rings with nonzero nilpotents.

For the divisibility input, Milne's quotient universal property is what matters: for n invertible and k algebraically closed, [n] is a separable isogeny and A[n] is reduced. Thus vanishing on the indicated geometric points implies vanishing on its kernel group scheme. Quotient by that kernel is A with quotient map [n], so a homomorphism killing the kernel descends uniquely. One must not impose the printed example's separability/isogeny assumption on f itself: f in the item is a-1 and might be zero or non-surjective. Remark 8.11(f)'s universal property gives exactly the arbitrary-homomorphism version stated in the new node, also used in the proof of 10.16 and 14.4(b).
