## Mathematical route and the new arithmetic node

Milne I Proposition 14.4(b), printed p.62, first writes alpha-1=n beta and uses integral eigenvalues and finite order, then invokes Lemma 14.5. That is the route here. The faithful left regular representation gives integer matrices U and B with U=I+nB and U^r=I. Cayley-Hamilton makes each eigenvalue (lambda-1)/n of B integral. Lemma 14.5 makes each eigenvalue lambda of U equal to one. Since X^r-1 has no repeated factor over C, U has invariant complements and hence its eigenspaces span. This gives U=I and, by evaluation at 1 in the regular representation, u=1. This is not a replacement by a congruence-subgroup theorem that already restates the item.

For the future worker of lem-sfb-algebraic-integer-root-unity-congruence, Milne's complete argument is available at I Lemma 14.5, printed p.63. If zeta is not one, choose a power eta of prime order p. The identity zeta^k-1=(zeta-1)(1+zeta+...+zeta^(k-1)) preserves divisibility by n by an algebraic integer, say eta-1=n beta. Beta lies in Q(eta), since beta=(eta-1)/n, and remains integral. The primitive p-th cyclotomic polynomial gives [Q(eta):Q]=p-1 and norm(eta-1)=(-1)^(p-1)p. Taking norms gives p=±n^(p-1)N(beta), with N(beta) an integer. For p=2 this says n divides 2; for p>=3 it says n^(p-1) divides p, impossible for n>=3. This proof is recorded here only, not smuggled into the unsplit child's statement. Mathlib contains cyclotomic norm formulas, but the reduction to prime order, integrality and divisibility contradiction require further work; none found is the child itself in one line.
